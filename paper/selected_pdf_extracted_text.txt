Abstract—Automatic translation evaluation is increasingly rel-
evant to multilingual public-service communication, where small
meaning changes can affect how a citizen’s request is inter-
preted. This paper presents a controlled challenge-set analysis
of COMETKiwi on synthetic English–Hindi citizen-grievance
translation pairs. The set contains 100 pairs across ten intended
administrative meaning-error categories, with one intended faith-
ful Hindi candidate and one deliberately corrupted candidate per
pair. We evaluate whether the metric assigns a higher quality-
estimation score to the faithful candidate. Across 100 pairs,
COMETKiwi ranked the faithful candidate higher in 95 cases
and the corrupted candidate higher in five, yielding 95% strict
pairwise agreement. The mean score difference was 0.041884,
and the median was 0.016785. Time-reference errors had the
lowest category-level agreement, with eight of ten pairs ranked
correctly. The exploratory analysis also examines candidate-
score distributions and pairwise score margins. These findings
characterize metric behavior on a small, synthetic, category-
balanced diagnostic set; they do not establish performance on
naturally occurring grievances or demonstrate real-world harm.
The pair labels reflect intended synthetic constructions and have
not been independently validated by bilingual human annotators.
Index Terms—machine translation evaluation, quality esti-

I. INTRODUCTION
Public-service systems increasingly receive requests in mul-
tiple languages. In this setting, translation errors involving
dates, amounts, locations, urgency, or requested actions may
change how a request is understood. General translation-
quality scores can summarize overall quality, but aggregate
scores may obscure whether an evaluator distinguishes a
faithful translation from a minimally altered candidate.
This study examines the pairwise ranking behavior of
COMETKiwi, a reference-free machine-translation quality-
estimation metric, on a controlled English–Hindi challenge
set designed around administrative meaning changes. Each
item contains an English source, an intended faithful Hindi
candidate, and a corrupted Hindi candidate intended to alter an
administrative detail. The experiment asks whether the metric
assigns a higher score to the faithful candidate.
The contributions are a synthetic, category-balanced set
of 100 candidate pairs, a pairwise and category-level score-
margin analysis, an exploratory analysis of candidate scores,
and an examination of five ranking inversions and low-margin
comparisons. This is a diagnostic analysis rather than a new
evaluation metric or a claim about deployment readiness.
Because the set is synthetic and its semantic labels have
not been independently validated by bilingual annotators, the
findings should be interpreted as a pilot characterization of
metric behavior.
II. RESEARCHQUESTIONS
This study addresses the following questions:
•RQ1:How often does COMETKiwi rank the intended
faithful Hindi candidate above the corrupted candidate?
•RQ2:How do pairwise ranking outcomes and score
margins vary across the ten intended error categories?
•RQ3:What characteristics are present in cases where the
corrupted candidate receives a higher score?
•RQ4:What do the candidate-score distributions and
pairwise margins reveal about metric behavior on this
constructed dataset?
III. RELATEDWORK
Automatic machine-translation evaluation has moved be-
yond lexical overlap toward learned metrics that model se-
mantic adequacy and translation quality. COMETKiwi pro-
vides reference-free quality estimation and was introduced in
the context of the WMT quality-estimation shared task [1].
Reference-free evaluation is useful when a gold reference
translation is unavailable, but its scores remain estimates rather
than guarantees of semantic correctness.
More recent work, including xCOMET, explores finer-
grained evaluation and error-span explanations [2]. Such work
motivates evaluating not only average scores but also behavior
on targeted error cases. For multilingual Indic settings, systems
such as IndicTrans2 illustrate ongoing efforts to improve
translation across Indian languages [3]. The present work is
complementary: it does not compare translation systems, but
probes whether a quality-estimation metric distinguishes in-
tended faithful and corrupted Hindi candidates in a constrained
administrative domain.
The study makes a narrow contribution: a controlled Hindi
citizen-grievance challenge-set analysis. It does not claim to

IV. EXPLORATORYDATAANALYSIS
A. Dataset Composition
The supplied result files contain 200 candidate-level scores
and 100 paired comparisons. Each pair contains one intended
faithful Hindi candidate and one corrupted candidate. The
challenge set is balanced by construction: each of the ten in-
tended error categories contains ten pairs, giving 20 candidate
translations per category.
The categories are negation and service status, date, du-
ration, amount, location, location detail, requested action,
department-routing correctness, time reference, and urgency.

B. Candidate Score Distributions
Across the 100 intended faithful candidates, the mean
COMETKiwi score was approximately 0.8557, with a standard
deviation of 0.0348. Across the 100 corrupted candidates, the
mean was approximately 0.8138, with a standard deviation of
0.0619. The observed score range across all candidates was
approximately 0.5597–0.8952.

Faithful candidates had a higher mean score in this dataset,
although the distributions overlap. A higher metric score does
not by itself establish semantic correctness.

D. EDA Summary
The exploratory analysis shows that the challenge set
contains ten pairs in each intended error category and that
intended faithful candidates have a higher mean COMETKiwi
score than corrupted candidates. However, some corrupted
candidates receive higher scores than their paired faithful
candidates. The score distributions overlap, and the score
margin varies across pairs.
These observations motivate the pairwise and category-level
analyses in Section VI. The dataset is synthetic, and its in-
tended semantic labels have not been independently validated
by bilingual human annotators. Therefore, this EDA describes
the constructed dataset and does not establish performance on

C. Analysis and V alidity
We report the strict pairwise outcome, mean and median
score differences, category-level outcomes, ranking inver-
sions, and counts of low-margin comparisons. The low-margin
thresholds are descriptive diagnostics rather than calibrated
confidence boundaries.
The dataset labels reflect the intended synthetic construc-
tion. No independent bilingual human validation or hu-
man inter-annotator agreement study was performed. An AI-
assisted semantic audit was used as a diagnostic aid, but it is
not ground truth and does not replace human validation.
Since pairs share category-specific construction patterns,
confidence intervals that assume independent observations
may not fully reflect dependence. The experiment also does
not measure the frequency of these errors in real-world data.
VI. RESULTS
A. Overall Pairwise Ranking
COMETKiwi assigned a higher score to the intended faith-
ful candidate in 95 of 100 pairs. In five pairs, the corrupted
candidate received a higher score; no ties were observed. The
strict pairwise agreement rate was therefore 95%.
The mean score difference was 0.041884, with a standard
deviation of 0.053249. The median was 0.016785. Observed
score differences ranged from−0.006049to0.301254.

Table II summarizes pairwise outcomes and score differ-

COMETKiwi ranked the intended faithful candidate higher

lowest agreement, with eight correct rankings out of ten.
The time-reference category had both the lowest strict
agreement and a small median margin. By contrast, the
negation/service-status category had a comparatively large
mean margin. These observations are descriptive and should
not be generalized beyond the current synthetic examples.
C. Ranking Inversions
The five ranking inversions had negative score differences
close to zero. Table III lists the affected pairs and their scores.
These examples are useful for follow-up inspection, but they
do not by themselves identify the mechanism behind a metric’s
ranking.
For all five inversions, the absolute score difference was
below 0.01. This indicates that the ranking errors in this run
were low-margin comparisons, although the scores alone can-
not establish why the metric preferred the corrupted candidate.
Potential explanations such as lexical overlap or contextual
representation effects require separate controlled analysis and
were not tested here.

VII. DISCUSSION
The main finding is that COMETKiwi ranked the intended
faithful candidate higher for most pairs in this synthetic
challenge set, while still producing five ranking inversions. The
inversions involved requested action, location, date, and time
reference. They illustrate why a high aggregate agreement rate
does not imply that every potentially important administrative
detail is reliably distinguished.
The category results caution against treating the score
as a uniform guarantee across error types. Time-reference
examples were the most difficult category in this run, whereas
all ten examples in six other categories were ranked as
intended. However, there are only ten examples per category,
and categories are defined by the set’s construction. These
differences motivate larger, independently validated follow-
up studies rather than strong claims about general category
difficulty.
The experiment is not a deployment evaluation. It does not
establish how frequently these errors occur in naturally sub-
mitted grievances, how a human operator would interpret the
examples, or whether a metric score would change a public-
service outcome. It also does not compare COMETKiwi with
human judgments or other metrics.
VIII. LIMITATIONS ANDETHICALCONSIDERATIONS
This pilot has several limitations. First, the 100 pairs are
synthetic and may not capture the linguistic variety, spelling
variation, code-switching, ambiguity, or contextual complexity
of real citizen grievances. Second, the intended labels have not
been independently verified by bilingual human annotators.
The AI-assisted audit is only a diagnostic tool and cannot be
presented as human validation or inter-annotator agreement.
Third, there are only ten pairs per category, limiting the
reliability of category-level estimates. Fourth, all examples
are evaluated with one metric checkpoint and one recorded
inference setup; the study does not establish comparative
performance against alternative evaluators. Fifth, the confi-
dence interval for the overall proportion does not account
for dependencies introduced by shared templates or category
construction.
The examples concern potentially consequential administra-
tive details, but the experiment measures metric rankings, not
actual harms. The paper therefore does not infer observed harm
or claim readiness for public-service deployment. Future work
should use independently authored examples, bilingual expert
review, adjudication of disputed labels, a larger sample, and
real or carefully de-identified examples where ethically and
legally appropriate. Any use in public services should retain
human oversight and should not use a metric score as the sole
basis for deciding how a grievance is handled.

X. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a controlled analysis of COMETKiwi
on 100 synthetic English–Hindi citizen-grievance candidate
pairs spanning ten intended administrative meaning-error cat-
egories. The metric ranked the intended faithful candidate
higher in 95 pairs, with five low-margin ranking inversions and
no ties. The time-reference category had the lowest pairwise
agreement in this set.
The exploratory analysis showed that the faithful candidates
had a higher mean score than corrupted candidates, while
their score distributions overlapped. These findings provide
a diagnostic snapshot of model behavior, not evidence of real-
world harm or reliable performance on naturally occurring
grievances. Independent bilingual validation, broader data,
baseline comparisons, and human evaluation are necessary
before drawing stronger conclusions about the use of quality-
estimation metrics in public-service workflows.
